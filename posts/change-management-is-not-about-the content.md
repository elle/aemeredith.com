# Change management is not about the content; it's about the context

> It is not the strongest of the species that survives, nor the most intelligent.
> It is the one most adaptable to change.
> ~ Charles Darwin

## The thing nobody tells you about change
Organisations don't actually change when you roll out a new system, redraw the org chart, or swap one process for another. Those are just the props. The real change happens, or doesn't, inside the people who have to live with the new arrangement.

You can install the shiniest software in the world, but if the people using it haven't made their own quiet internal shift, you've changed nothing. You've just spent a lot of money rearranging furniture.

So if you're leading change, your real job isn't managing the rollout. It's helping people through a personal transition that can be genuinely hard, loss of status, loss of comfort, and sometimes loss of a job. The smoother you make that human journey, the faster the benefits actually land. There's no shortcut around the people part.

## Why change happens (and why the "why" matters)
Change usually shows up in one of two flavours. There's the slow, steady kind, continuous tinkering and refinement that barely registers as change at all. And there's the dramatic kind, the big-bang reorganisation that everyone feels at once, usually because something forced the issue.

Either way, change should be in service of where the organisation is genuinely trying to go. It tends to touch everything: people, processes, clients, structure, technology, so it's worth being honest about your current state versus your desired one before you start moving things around. Change for the sake of looking decisive is just disruption with better branding.

## A word on frameworks
You'll find no shortage of models that promise to make this simple. Kotter has eight steps. Knoster has five inputs — vision, skills, incentives, resources, and a plan. Knoster’s point is a good one: pull out any single ingredient, and you get a predictable flavour of failure. No vision, and people are confused. No skills, and they're anxious. No incentive, and they resist. No resources, and they get frustrated. No plan, and you get a string of false starts.

That's a genuinely useful checklist, and I'd keep it in your back pocket. But here's my honest take: nothing this complicated is ever as tidy as a cookie-cutter framework. The boxes are clean; the people inside them are not. The models tell you what to have in place. They're quieter on the messier question of why a perfectly resourced, well-planned change still curdles. For that, you have to look at what's happening in the group.

## The real barrier: what groups do under threat
Kübler-Ross suggests a grief cycle has various stages: denial, anger, bargaining, depression, and acceptance. The cycle maps surprisingly well onto how individuals respond to change being done to them. People really do bargain. They really do go quiet and flat before they come round.

But individuals aren't grieving in isolation. They're doing it inside a group, and groups under threat behave in ways that no individual would own up to.

When a group feels anxious, and change always makes a group anxious, it stops working on the actual task and starts unconsciously working on its own survival instead. You've seen the symptoms even if you never had a name for them:

- The rescue fantasy. Everyone goes a bit passive and waits for the leader to swoop in with The Answer. Meetings fill with "what does management want us to do?" because depending on a saviour is more comfortable than facing the uncertainty themselves.
- Find an enemy. The group bonds by uniting against a threat: the new system, the consultants, "head office," that one department. Fighting a common villain is a relief, because it's easier than sitting with your own discomfort.
- The endless wait. Energy drains into hoping things will sort themselves out, a reorg that never lands, a saviour hire, the magic future state where everything's fine. Anything but focusing on their main task and the work in front of them.

None of this is people being difficult on purpose. It's a group managing its own fear, mostly without realising it. And it's exactly the stuff your tidy five-box model doesn't capture, because the resistance isn't usually about the content of the change at all. It's about the anxiety the change has stirred up in the room.

Which is the whole point: manage the context, not just the content. If you only argue the merits of the new process, you'll lose, because you're answering a question nobody's actually asking. The thing people are really reacting to is the threat to how the group holds together. Name that, sit with it, and make it safe to be uncertain out loud, and the content takes care of itself.

## Talk. Then talk more. Then talk again.
If there's one place leaders consistently under-invest, it's communication. The instinct is to send the big announcement, feel relieved it's done, and move on. But you have to remember what's actually happening in the room: an anxious group is only half-listening. People are too busy managing their own worry to absorb much, so the first time you say something, a good chunk of it simply doesn't land. The message isn't really received until it's been heard several times, from a few directions, once the initial fear has settled enough for people to take it in.

So the rule of thumb is to communicate far more than feels necessary, to the point where you're slightly bored of saying it. That's usually about when other people are hearing it for the first time. Repetition isn't nagging here; it's how you signal that the change is real, that it isn't quietly going to be reversed, and that you're not going to disappear the moment things get uncomfortable. Consistency over time is itself reassuring to an anxious group.

And it matters how you say it, not just how often. Different messages need different mediums, and different people take things in differently:

- The big set-piece: the town hall, the all-hands, the launch email, is good for the headline and the why. But it's the worst possible place to expect honesty back from people. Nobody airs their real fears in a room of two hundred.
- Small groups and team huddles are where the actual work happens. This is where people will admit what's worrying them, push back, and start to make the change their own. Protect this space and show up to it yourself.
- One-to-ones are where you reach the people quietly carrying the most, the ones who've gone silent, or who stand to lose status. A five-minute corridor conversation often does more than any deck.
- Written, durable stuff: a short FAQ, a running update, a single page people can actually find, matters because anxious people forget. They need somewhere to go back and check rather than relying on what they think they heard.
- Informal channels: the chat message, the quick voice note, the "just so you know", keep the change feeling alive between the formal moments, so it doesn't go quiet and let the rumour mill fill the silence.

The mix matters because no single channel reaches everyone, and a group under stress is quick to read meaning into silence. If they don't hear from you, they'll invent a story, and it's rarely a flattering one. Over-communicating across a few different mediums is how you crowd out that vacuum before it fills itself.

And listen at least as much as you broadcast. Communication that only flows outward is just announcements. The point is to give the group a legitimate place to put its anxiety into honest conversation with you, rather than into resistance, gossip, or quietly waiting for the whole thing to fail.

## So what actually helps
None of this needs to be complicated. A lot of leading change well comes down to a handful of unglamorous habits, most of which are about managing how the group is feeling rather than the mechanics of the rollout.

Start by being clear about why this is happening, in a way people can genuinely believe rather than a tidy phrase that sounds good on a slide. Make sure the people at the top are visibly behind it, not just named on a document, but actually showing up, because the group reads that as a signal about whether the change is for real.

Get to know who's affected and be honest with yourself about who stands to lose something, because those are the people whose worry will quietly drive the mood. Don't try to be the lone hero who has all the answers; pull a few people together to carry it with you, partly so the group has someone other than you to turn to. Keep an eye on whether things are actually working, and when something goes right early on, say so out loud. Small wins are the best antidote to the story that this is all going to be a disaster.

Through all of it, stay honest, especially when the news is awkward, and make it safe for people to try things, get them wrong, and say what they really think. Reward the people who lean in and engage, not just the ones who happen to hit a target.

If you look back over that list, almost none of it is about systems or processes. It's about reading the group's mood and tending to it. That's the whole game: get the context right, and the people will carry the content for you.
